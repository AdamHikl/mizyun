# Tracking engagement psychology — 2026-10-17

Research only. No code, config or product change was made. Context: users log meals
for about two days, drop off for one to two days, then return (issue #292 forgiving
weekly goal and welcome-back card; #293 vegetarian protein range). Product stance:
durable, enjoyable habits, not compulsion.

Verification method: each source below was looked up on PubMed, Crossref or a
publisher/official page on 2026-10-17. "Verified" means title, authors, venue, year
and the quoted numbers matched the abstract. Where only existence was confirmed, or
a number came from a secondary summary, the row says so. Nothing here is Mizyun
usage data; no claim has been tested on Mizyun users.

## 1. Executive summary

**What works (with the caveat that most trials are short and in physical activity,
not food logging).**

- Logging often is associated with better weight outcomes, but the link is
  observational and logging effort falls fast over time (S4, S5, S6, S7).
- A missed day barely changes habit formation; consistency in a stable context
  matters more than perfection (S1).
- Gamified activity programmes beat controls by a small-to-medium margin during the
  programme, and gains fade after it ends (S10, S12, S13). Goal design and
  self-chosen goals matter (S14).
- Fresh-start moments (week, month, birthday) raise goal initiation (S18).
- Lower logging effort (photo or quick logging) is the most direct lever on
  adherence for a food app (S6, S7, S8).

**What backfires.**

- Highlighting a broken streak lowers later engagement, independent of actual
  past behaviour (S17). Reset-to-zero messaging is a documented demotivator.
- Rigid, all-or-nothing rules invite the "what-the-hell" disinhibition seen in
  dieters (S19).
- Tangible, expected, engagement-contingent rewards undermine intrinsic motivation
  (d about -0.4) in the lab literature (S25).
- Quantified food and exercise tracking is associated with more eating-disorder
  symptoms, and people with eating disorders often attribute harm to calorie apps
  (S28 to S32). Evidence is cross-sectional or qualitative, not causal.
- Variable-reward and loss-aversion mechanics are the same levers used in
  compulsion-oriented design (S27).

**Top 5 recommendations for Mizyun.**

1. Keep the forgiving weekly goal ("X of Y days", no reset) as the primary
   consistency frame; never display a lost streak or a zero.
2. Make logging cheaper before making it more rewarding: photo-first, "copy
   yesterday", quick re-log, and a "good enough" partial log that counts.
3. Use weekly fresh starts and welcome-back copy that frames a return as normal
   (no guilt, no count of missed days).
4. Reward the behaviour of logging and completing a week, never calories, weight,
   deficit or "perfect" days; keep rewards non-tangible and optional.
5. Ship an ED-safety layer: calorie numbers and weight shown only on request in a
   softer mode, a one-tap "hide numbers" setting, no streak-pressure notifications,
   and a route to support if risk signals appear.

are involved.

## 4. Guardrails for Mizyun

Product rules derived from the evidence above. These are proposals for the founder, not
implemented behaviour.

1. No reset-to-zero messaging anywhere. After a gap, show what the user has (days
   logged this week, month, lifetime), not what they lost (S17, S19).
2. No reward, badge, point or celebration for lower calories, lower weight, a larger
   deficit or a "clean" day (S31, S32).
3. No variable-reward or surprise mechanics; all rewards are predictable and
   explained (S27).
4. Notifications: user-chosen time, at most one per day, no streak-at-risk or guilt
   copy, no "you missed N days", easy global off, quiet after 2 ignored days.
5. Return copy treats a gap as normal ("Welcome back; your week starts fresh").
6. Rewards are non-tangible and optional; no purchase of streak protection (S25).
7. Opt-out everywhere: hide streak/goal widgets, hide calorie and weight numbers,
   turn off celebrations (autonomy, S24, S26).
8. ED-risk safeguards: screen lightly at onboarding without diagnosing; if a user
   reports an ED history or enters extreme targets (very low intake, rapid weight
   loss), switch to a non-numeric mode, show support resources, and do not offer
   challenges or deficit goals. Excluding users is a policy decision for the
   founder and clinical advice; this document provides none.
9. Frame goals around health and function (energy, protein, consistency), not weight
   or body shape (S31).
10. Cap logging burden: photo-first, copy-yesterday, "good enough" partial logs that
    still count (S5, S6, S8).
11. Social features opt-in, small groups, no food comparisons (S32, S13).
12. Vegetarian protein copy: encouraging ranges, no "deficient" or red state; point
    to variety and total pattern, not a single number (S35, S36, S37).
13. Measure, don't assume: A/B or staged-rollout any game element and track wellbeing
    proxies (opt-out rate, number-hiding use), not just retention.

## 5. Sources, dates and limits

Accessed 2026-10-17. Verified means a title, authors, venue and year match from
PubMed, Crossref, the publisher or a fetched page.

**Association vs causal evidence.**

- Causal (randomised): S6 (pilot), S12, S13, S14 (activity trials); S35 (small RCT);
  S10 and S34 (meta-analyses of RCTs); S22 and S23 (field experiments); S17
  (randomised online experiments).
- Associational only: S4, S5, S7 (logging vs outcomes and attrition); S18 (archival
  field data); S28 to S31 (cross-sectional harm studies); S26 (correlational
  meta-analysis).
- Qualitative or conceptual: S2, S3 (theory), S9, S24, S27, S32.
- Company-reported: S20 (Duolingo A/B test, no CI, not peer reviewed).
- No source here shows that food-logging gamification causes or prevents eating
  disorders. The harm studies cannot rule out that people with existing symptoms
  choose trackers.
- Almost all gamification trials measure steps, not food logging.
